What you get is the ability to ask a question and get an answer. Not perfect attribution, which nobody has, but enough instrumentation that whether the work is landing stops being a matter of opinion.

Most of what looks like a marketing problem two years into a partnership turns out to have been an infrastructure problem the whole time.

That means a website, the connections between the tools you already run, and the measurement layer underneath both. It is the least romantic chapter and the one that determines whether the next three years of publishing produce anything you can point to. A story with no engine behind it stays in a document.

The framing that matters here is faithfulness. Everything built in this chapter exists to take one settled message and reproduce it accurately across a thousand small surfaces—a page, a form, a confirmation email, a tracking parameter on a link somebody clicked at ten at night.

## The artifact

Chapter 2 closes with a [website](/system/artifact) that is live, indexed, and wired to measure. All three conditions, not just the first. A site that launched but does not appear in search, or launched but cannot tell you who arrived and from where, has not finished the chapter.

## When this happens

Chapter 2 typically lands in months two through five, depending on the size of the build. The website is the long run and the one with the most review cycles in it.

There is an important exception. If you arrive with no CRM and no analytics worth the name, we will often pull § 2.2 and § 2.3 ahead of the website. Instrumenting is cheaper and faster than designing, it does not wait on approval cycles, and it starts the measurement baseline in week two instead of month five. Building the site first and instrumenting afterward costs you most of a year of evidence.

After the artifact, § 2.1 becomes a steady trickle of updates and landing pages, § 2.2 gets touched whenever your stack changes, and § 2.3 runs continuously for the life of the partnership.

## What this chapter asks of you

Less of your time than Chapter 1, and more of your patience.

The two things that reliably delay this chapter are both administrative. Credentials for third-party systems—the reliable delay in an integration project is almost never the build, it is waiting on admin access to two platforms nobody remembers the password for. And review turnaround on the website, where scattered feedback across many rounds is what turns a six-week build into a five-month one.

A note on calendars. Domain and DNS work carries mandatory waiting periods that no amount of urgency shortens—sometimes servers need a few days to publish a site.
