Before we propose anything, we write you a thesis.

It is one page. It argues why this partnership should exist, and it is written as a claim that can be wrong. That last property is the entire point. A claim that can be wrong has to name what would prove it wrong, and naming that is what turns a document into an anchor.

Everything downstream inherits its argument from this page. The proposal prices work. The messaging document defines language. The go-to-market plan sequences activity. The monthly audit reports movement. None of them say why any of it should be happening at all. The thesis does, and it becomes the standard the rest gets measured against.

## Why one page

Four hundred to five hundred words, hard cap.

The limit is a quality mechanism rather than a formatting preference. At that length there is no room to describe two buyers, so we have to pick one. No room to hedge the arithmetic, so it either lands on a finding or it says nothing. No room for a long list of unknowns, so only the ones that actually block the claim survive. The constraint does the editorial work, and a thesis that needs two pages is a thesis that has not been decided yet.

## What is on the page

Six moves, in this order, because each depends on the one before it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The situation">
    Where your revenue comes from today, with numbers. The machine as it currently runs, not the company history.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The buyer">
    One kind of person, described by what they believe, and what they are shopping for regardless of who sells it to them. If you have two buyers, we pick one and say why.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The gap">
    Why that buyer does not currently arrive. This is the problem being purchased, stated as one mechanism rather than a list of weaknesses.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The bet">
    Where we reach them, in what form, at what cadence, expressed in § coordinates. This is what makes the scope conversation legible a year later.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The arithmetic">
    Derived, not asserted. Your revenue goal divided by your unit value gives required customers. Divided by your close rate gives required conversations. Divided by your hand-raise rate gives required prospects, which gives required reach. We show the division.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The conditions">
    What must be true, who owns which part, and one sentence beginning "This thesis is wrong if."
  </Step>
</Steps>

The falsification line is the most important sentence on the page, and it is the one no competitor will write for you.

## What it is not

It has no deliverable table, no calendar, no price, and no tactics list. The moment it has a deliverable table it is a proposal, and the moment it has a calendar it is a go-to-market plan. Both exist downstream and both are better at their jobs.

It is also not a diagnosis with a prescription attached. The thesis names what is broken. It does not explain how to fix it. That is a deliberate line, and it is what keeps the second conversation an argument about your business rather than a free consulting session.

## Why the conditions section is load-bearing

We do not control your price, your product, your close rate, or who answers the phone when a lead comes in. The conditions section names which parts of the arithmetic are ours and which are yours, before anyone is disappointed.

Without it, the thesis quietly makes us liable for an outcome we can only partly cause. With it, month nine becomes a specific conversation—the reach happened, the conversations happened, the close rate did not—which is a very different meeting than a vague argument about whether marketing is working.

## The three states

The thesis is a manuscript in miniature. It has a draft, a revision, and a final form.

| State | When | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Draft | Written from the discovery conversation, delivered in person | Underwritten by judgment, and it says so on the page |
| Revised | At the Storybook capstone | The buyer and gap sections get rewritten from the messaging interviews, because that is where we learn the first draft was wrong |
| Standing | When Scale's baseline period closes | The arithmetic finally carries your real numbers, and every audit measures against it |

At the Epilogue the thesis is rendered as confirmed, disconfirmed, or amended, with the full stack of revisions behind it. That stack is the intellectual history of the partnership, and it is what makes a handover feel like a finished book rather than a folder of files.

<Note>
  We expect the first draft to be wrong about the buyer. The revision at the Storybook capstone is the plan, not a concession. Being corrected out loud is the mechanism.
</Note>

## What we will ask you

Six facts, asked in the same order every time. They are worth knowing before the conversation, because most companies discover they cannot answer all of them, and that discovery is frequently the finding the thesis is built on.

- The source of each of your last ten customers, one at a time
- Your close rate from first conversation to signed
- Average customer value and average tenure
- Sales cycle length
- What you spent on marketing last year and what it produced
- Whether your website can identify who arrived and where they came from

If most of that comes back empty, we do not write you a thesis with invented arithmetic. We write one whose bet is instrumentation itself, testable in ninety days. Fiction dressed as forecasting is the thing this whole exercise exists to escape.

We also add one question specific to your industry—the one that could invalidate the entire bet. For a fund, whether the offering permits general solicitation. For a regulated service, what claims are permitted. For a channel business, who owns the customer relationship.

## How it gets written

We have a discovery conversation and record it. We draft from that conversation over the following few days. Then we deliver the draft in person—never by email—and open with some version of: here is what I think is true about your business, and here is what I would bet on. Tell me where I am wrong.

Agreement is a conversation or an email. There is no signature. A countersigned thesis reads contractual, and this is not a contract.

## Who has to be in the room

The CEO, or whoever carries revenue accountability. Nobody else can answer a question about close rate or pipeline, so we decline the meeting rather than hold it with the wrong person.
