A brand that sounds careful and looks careless is making two claims, and people believe the second one. Visual identity sits inside Story rather than alongside it because how a company looks is part of what it says, not a decoration applied afterward.

Which is also why this section comes after § 1.1. A visual identity built before the message is settled is a designer guessing at a personality that has not yet been decided. Once the message exists, the design brief nearly writes itself—the adjectives are already in the messaging document, and the audience research already told us what this particular buyer trusts the look of.

## Which one you need

The question is not whether your current identity is good. It is whether it is pointing in the right direction.

If it is directionally right and simply inconsistent—four versions of the logo in circulation, a palette nobody wrote down, decks that each invented their own typography—that is a [refresh](/story/visual-identity/brand-refresh), and it is a fraction of the work.

If it is pointing somewhere the company no longer is, sharpening it makes the wrong direction sharper. That requires a [full identity](/story/visual-identity/brand-identity). We will tell you which one we think you have, and we are more often arguing for the refresh than against it.

## How the work is split

We write the brief, our design lead executes it in Figma, and you review at defined points rather than continuously. The brief covers brand personality with a visual definition for each adjective, where the brand sits between minimal and expressive and between classic and modern, color and typography direction, imagery direction, and a set of explicit do-and-don’t pairs so that “make it feel warmer” has a shared meaning before anyone opens a file.

The pattern that wastes the most time in design work is feedback arriving as adjectives with no shared referent. The brief exists to prevent that, and the review points exist so you are never surprised by a direction three weeks after it was set.
